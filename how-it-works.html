3  How the study works, step by step

Every step shown with a real example, plus a list of the words used in this report

In short. A research question goes through eight steps before it becomes an AI answer: key words, automatic suggestions, the official thesaurus, three searches, the Finna index, a check of why each record was found, the AI answer, and a check of the answer’s citations. This page follows one real question through all eight: How did representations of women in Finnish advertising change between 1920 and 1960?

1. The question and its key words

Each question comes with two to four key words chosen by the researcher. They are the core ideas that must appear in the evidence. In this report they are also called anchors.

  • Question: How did representations of women in Finnish advertising change between 1920 and 1960?
  • Key words: women and advertising
  • Years: 1920-1960

2. Annif suggests subject words

Annif is a tool made by the National Library of Finland. It reads a text and suggests subject words from the official thesaurus, as a cataloguer would. It gives each suggestion a confidence score between 0 and 1.

We reach Annif through our R package annif (version 0.1.1), which sends the question to the public Finto AI service and only reads its answer. Annif’s models are retrained from time to time, so the run records which model answered: yso-en (backend nn_ensemble, model last changed 2025-09-16); yso-fi (backend nn_ensemble, model last changed 2025-09-16).

We keep a suggestion only if it fits one of our key words. It fits when it is the same idea, a closely related idea in the thesaurus, or shares the start of a word with it (advertising and advertisements). A suggestion that is only a piece of a key word phrase (Finland for Finland Swedes) is not added, because it would make the search wider than the idea. Suggestions that fit none of our words are written down but not searched. They show how the computer would have framed the question differently.

3. The official thesaurus gives the standard names

YSO (the General Finnish Ontology) is the national list of subject words that Finnish libraries, archives and museums use to describe their collections. Each idea has one official name in Finnish, Swedish and English, plus links to wider, narrower and related ideas. We look ideas up through the Finto service.

4. Building the three searches

Every search word has a source: you, Annif or the thesaurus. Search A uses only your words, B adds Annif’s, and C adds the thesaurus names on top. Words for the same idea are joined with OR; the ideas are joined with AND.

(women OR “view of women” OR naiset OR kvinnor OR naiskuva OR kvinnobilden) AND (advertising OR mainonta OR reklam)

5. Finna answers

Finna is the shared search service of Finnish libraries, archives and museums. For each search it reports how many records in its whole index match (we call this the size of the search), and returns the best-matching records in its own order. We keep the top 60 from each search, so all three searches get the same amount of space.

Search Search words Matching records in Finna Records kept (max 60)
A: own words 2 245 60
B: + Annif 3 245 60
C: + YSO terms 9 1150 60

Because each search keeps only 60 records, a wider search does not just add records: it can also push out records that a narrower search would have shown.

6. Why was each record found?

For every record we look for the search words in its title, subject words and description. That tells us which step brought it in:

  • your words: one of your own key words appears;
  • Annif: it only matches through a word Annif suggested;
  • thesaurus: it only matches through an official name added in search C (often Finnish or Swedish);
  • hidden: none of the words is visible in the parts of the record we can see. Finna searches more fields than it shows us, so we cannot tell why it matched.
Search Your words Annif Thesaurus Hidden
A: own words 60 0 0 0
B: + Annif 60 0 0 0
C: + YSO terms 57 0 3 0

7. The AI writes an answer

The top 8 records of each search go to an AI model (here from OpenAI), with strict rules: use only these records, say when the evidence is too thin, and put the record numbers after every sentence that says something about the records. So each search gets its own answer.

Two sentences from the answer for search A:

A 1921 advertising record is catalogued as a political advertisement linked to a women’s organisation titled “Me odotamme sinua” (Social Democratic Women’s Union). [werstas.we-1372484]

A 1950 poster record is catalogued with subjects that include women’s rights movements and the cooperative system. [ta_ah.ta-1369756]

8. Checking the answer

We split every answer into sentences and check, automatically:

  • whether the sentence cites any records;
  • whether the cited records really were among the 8 the AI received (if not, it made them up);
  • which search step brought the cited records in, and whether search A could have found them.

What a computer cannot check is whether the records really support what the sentence says. That is the job of the human checking.

Words used in this report

Finna
The national search service for Finnish libraries, archives and museums. All records in this study come from it.
Annif
An automatic subject-indexing tool from the National Library of Finland, offered as the public service Finto AI. It suggests subject words for a text. We use it through the R package annif.
YSO
The General Finnish Ontology: the official list of subject words, with names in Finnish, Swedish and English.
Finto
The online service where YSO and other Finnish vocabularies are published. We use it to look up YSO.
Key word (anchor)
One of the two to four core ideas the researcher picks for a question, e.g. women and advertising.
Search A, B, C
A uses only your key words. B adds Annif’s suggestions that fit them. C adds YSO’s official names in three languages.
Query
The exact text sent to Finna. AND means both ideas must appear; OR means any of the listed words will do.
Matching records (size of the search)
How many records in the whole Finna index match a search. It shows how wide the search is.
Records kept
The top 60 records of a search, in Finna’s order. All comparisons use these.
Evidence
The top 8 records that the AI reads before writing its answer.
Overlap
How many records two searches have in common. We use the Jaccard share: records found by both, divided by records found by either. 1 means the same records, 0 means none shared.
Rank overlap (RBO)
Like overlap, but it cares most about the top of the list. Two searches that agree on their first few records score high even if they differ further down.
Difference in mix (JSD)
The Jensen-Shannon divergence. A number from 0 to 1 that compares two mixes, for example of institutions. 0 = exactly the same mix; 1 = nothing in common. Around 0.1 is a small change; above 0.5 is a big one.
Uncertainty range
Because we only look at 60 records, a mix could look different just by chance. The range shows the values that are believable given the records we have.
Diversity
How many different institutions (or languages) a search shows, counting rare ones less. Ten equally common institutions give a diversity of 10.
Hidden match
A record that matches a search, but none of the search words appear in the parts of the record that Finna shows us.
Claim
One sentence of an AI answer.
Citation
A record number in square brackets after a sentence, for example [werstas.we-1372484].
Blinded checking
The checkers see the records and sentences mixed up, without knowing which search they came from, so their judgement is fair.
